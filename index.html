Ramp is the invoice nobody reads

Every senior hire tows an invisible invoice: the salary that runs while output is still learning the codebase. The offer letter starts the meter, the first fully productive week arrives months later, and none of the intervening payroll shows up in any comparison shoppers run. Subscription pricing has the opposite shape, because the vendor absorbs the ramp: onboarding is measured in hours and the first delivery in days. Put the two meters side by side and hiring's real unit is the quarter, not the month.

Take the fully loaded figure the hiring market runs on: $18,290 a month for a senior US developer. Say full speed arrives after three months, a generous ramp, with half output in month one and three quarters in month two. The dead weight is $9,145 plus $4,572.50, which is $13,717.50 paid for no net output, all inside $54,870 paid before day 90. None of it is waste in the hiring sense, but it is cost the flat-fee lane never charges, because the vendor's bench is already warm.

The subscription lane runs the same quarter on a different clock. Onboarding is about 24 hours to a live Trello board, then the first request inside the 72-hour turnaround claim, on the $5,995 monthly plan. One subscription day is $199.83, so onboarding is a $200 line and first code consumes four subscription-days, about $800 of subscription time. First code lands by day 4, about 86 days before the hire reaches full speed on day 90. And while the queue of request cards waits its turn the meter reads zero: one active card at a time, unlimited cards at no marginal salary. During ramp the hire's meter bills $609.67 a calendar day regardless.
